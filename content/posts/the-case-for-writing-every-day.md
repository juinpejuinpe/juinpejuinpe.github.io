People often ask writers for the secret. They expect something mystical — a
ritual, a particular pen, a candle lit at dawn. The truth is more boring and
more difficult: write every day.

## The myth of waiting for inspiration

Inspiration feels like a gift, so we treat it as one: something that arrives
unannounced and cannot be summoned. But waiting for it is a trap. The writer
who waits becomes someone who talks about writing, plans to write, and
eventually — quietly — stops calling themselves a writer at all.

Routine is the opposite of waiting. It's a decision made in advance, so that
on the days when motivation is absent, the work happens anyway.

## Why daily matters more than much

An hour on Sunday cannot replace twenty minutes on a Tuesday. Daily practice
keeps the channel open. Sentences from yesterday are still warm when you sit
down today. Ideas that seemed dead at midnight surprise you by morning.

Daily writing also lowers the stakes. If you write every day, no single
session has to be good. Today's failure is merely tomorrow's draft — and
tomorrow is guaranteed to arrive.

## Ten bad sentences

The bar is deliberately low: ten sentences. Some days you'll clear it and
keep going for two hours. Some days you'll write exactly ten sentences and
delete nine of them. Both count. Both are writing.

The goal was never to produce a masterpiece every morning. The goal is to
remain in conversation with the page long enough that a masterpiece — or at
least a decent paragraph — becomes possible.

So tomorrow morning, before the phone, before the news: ten sentences. The
muse can find you there; she knows the address by now.
